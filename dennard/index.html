How to use this page

This page has two parts. The demo guide comes first. It covers both demos you run. The slide content comes second. It covers all 33 slides in order.

Everything is organised by heading. The demo guide has one heading per task. The slide content has one heading per slide, numbered in order. Under each slide heading is the text on that slide, then a description of the picture. Use the headings rotor if you want to move around that way.

Some slides repeat the point of the slide before them. Those slides use the same heading with "continued" added. You will hear the same heading two or three times in a row. This is on purpose. It means the point has not changed.

The slide pictures are marked decorative, so they are skipped. Everything in them is written out in the text. Nothing on this page moves or plays on its own.

The two live sites are the bad version of Let's Play and the improved version.

Demo guide

You run the demo twice. The first demo is on the bad site, after slide 3. The second is on the improved site, at slide 33. You do the same four tasks both times.

Adeline prompts you at the start of each task. You work through it out loud while the room watches your screen. The notes under each task list what the page does, so you can name each problem as you meet it.

Demo on the bad site

This demo runs on the bad version of Let's Play, straight after slide 3.

Part 1: First impressions of the home page

Adeline asks: can you open the home page and tell us what you understand from it?

What you will run into:

  • The logo announces as "logo underscore one dot s v g". The filename is the alt text.
  • The hamburger announces as "button" and nothing else. It has no name. It does not say whether it is open or closed.
  • The hero is a three-slide carousel. It advances on its own while you are reading it.
  • There are no headings until well down the page. The first ones are level 4. There is no level 1 or level 2 above them.
  • There are no landmarks, so you cannot jump to the events or the footer.
  • The event photos have no alt attribute, so you may hear the image path read out.
  • Three links all say "Register" and three more all say "Read More".
  • The three social links in the footer announce as "link", "link", "link".
  • The whole navigation appears twice in the links list, because there are two copies of it in the page.

Part 2: Task 1: find Saturday Reservoir Run and register for Sat, 5 Jun

What you will run into, in order:

  • The three events are plain containers, not a list. Nothing announces "list, 3 items". There is no boundary between one event and the next. To find which "Register" belongs to Saturday Reservoir Run, you have to read everything in between.
  • Its dates read as "Sat, five slash six, twelve slash six, nineteen slash six". The middle dots between the date, the time and the location are spoken as punctuation.
  • On the registration page, the three form fields have no labels. The only hint is the placeholder text. The asterisk that marks a field as required is in the placeholder too, so both disappear once you type.
  • The three date options look like radio buttons but are plain containers. There is no group and no announced count. Nothing tells you which one is selected. Sat 5/6 is already selected by default.
  • The terms and conditions checkbox is also a plain container. It never says checked or unchecked.
  • The "Verify you're human" control has to be dragged sideways. That gesture conflicts with the swipe you use to move between elements.
  • Submitting the form shows a confirmation on screen. It is never announced, so you cannot tell whether it worked.

Part 3: Task 2: find the news story "Volunteer coaches needed for spring term"

What you will run into:

  • It is the third of three news items. The news section is a carousel that rotates about once a second.
  • The only controls are three dots. They are unlabelled buttons and all announce the same. Nothing marks which one is current.
  • Choosing a dot does not stop the rotation. It moves on again a moment after you reach the item.
  • There is no pause, stop or hide control anywhere.

Part 4: Task 3: open the navigation and go to Contact Us

What you will run into:

  • The hamburger is a plain container styled to look like a button. You can reach it and open it. It announces as "button" with no name and no state, so nothing tells you the menu opened.
  • Opening it does not move focus. The menu markup sits at the very end of the page. Moving forward from the hamburger takes you into the hero, the events, the spotlight and the news first. On screen, the menu is covering all of that.
  • The content behind the panel is not made unreachable, so you can still reach all of it.
  • When you reach the menu, Contact Us appears twice. The navigation exists twice in the page.

Demo on the improved site

This demo runs on the improved version of Let's Play, at slide 33. The four tasks are the same. For each one, point out which problem from the first demo has gone.

Part 1: First impressions of the home page

Adeline asks: can you open the home page again and tell us what you understand from it this time?

What is different:

  • A "Skip to main content" link is the first thing you reach, so you can jump past the header in one move.
  • The logo announces as "Let's Play home" instead of a filename.
  • The menu button announces as "Open menu". It also says whether it is expanded or collapsed.
  • There are real landmarks. A header, a navigation region named "Primary", one main region and a footer. You can move between them instead of swiping through everything.
  • The headings form a proper outline. One level 1, "Every Body Belongs". Level 2 for each section. Level 3 for each event and news item. The headings rotor now gives you the shape of the page.
  • The hero is a single still image with its text underneath it, so nothing moves while you are reading.
  • The three events are a real list. You hear "list, 3 items". Each event's title, status and link are grouped as one item.
  • Each event's status is spoken as "Spots available" or "Fully booked". It is no longer shown only as a coloured dot.
  • Every link says where it goes. The three links called "Register" are now named after their events, such as "Register for Saturday Reservoir Run".
  • The footer's social links announce as "Let's Play on Facebook", "Let's Play on Instagram" and "Let's Play on YouTube".
  • The navigation appears once, not twice.

Part 2: Task 1: find Saturday Reservoir Run and register for Sat, 5 Jun

What is different:

  • The link is called "Register for Saturday Reservoir Run", so you can find it in one move in the links rotor.
  • The event details read as one sentence. "Saturdays, 5, 12 and 19 Jun, 8:00 to 9:00 AM, at Serangoon Reservoir". No slashes and no middle dots.
  • The registration page is named after the event you came from. It reads "Event Registration for Saturday Reservoir Run".
  • Every field has a real label. Each label says "(required)" or "(optional)" in its own text, so that does not disappear when you type.
  • The dates are a real radio group. Its legend is "Select the date you would like to attend". You hear the group name, the number of options and which one is selected. The dates are the Saturday dates for this event, not a fixed set of Tuesdays.
  • The terms and conditions control is a real checkbox. It announces whether it is checked.
  • The "Slide to verify" control is gone. A background check replaces it and needs no interaction.
  • The confirmation after submitting is announced. You hear it at the same moment it appears on screen.

Part 3: Task 2: find the news story "Volunteer coaches needed for spring term"

What is different:

  • The news carousel is gone. All three stories are in a plain list and all are present at once.
  • Each headline is a real level 3 heading. You can go straight to it from the headings rotor.
  • Its link says "Read about volunteering as a coach this spring" instead of "Read More".
  • Each news photo has alt text describing what is in it.

Part 4: Task 3: open the navigation and go to Contact Us

What is different:

  • The menu button is a real button with a name. Opening it changes the name to "Close menu" and the state to expanded. Focus stays on the button, so you hear that change where you already are.
  • The menu markup sits straight after the header. The next move takes you into the first menu item.
  • When the menu is closed it is properly hidden. Its links are not left in the page.
  • Contact Us appears once.
  • Closing the menu returns focus to the button you opened it with.

Slide 2: The bad example

On the slide: "The bad example". Let's Play is a fictional community sports club website. "Scan to access:" above a QR code.

Shown: on the right, a phone-width screenshot of the bad site's home page. The Let's Play logo and a hamburger icon sit in the header. Slide 3: Discussion: what visual accessibility issues can you find?

On the slide: "Discussion:" then "What visual accessibility issues can you find?" Three minutes, with the person next to you:

  • Is there enough contrast between text and its background?
  • Am I relying on colour alone to communicate meaning?
  • Are labels clear?
  • Are buttons and controls large enough to identify and target?
  • Can I tell where I need to click?
  • Can I tell whether something is selected, focused, disabled, or active?

Shown: text only.

Your demo starts after this slide. By this point the room has spent three minutes looking at the bad page, so they will have found the problems they can see. You pick it up from there, following the demo on the bad site in the demo guide above.

1. Colour

Slide 4: Use enough contrast between text and its background

On the slide: "1. Colour", then the heading. Contrast ratios should be at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text.

Shown: the bad site's hero, a photo of two runners, one a blindfolded athlete running with a guide. White text reading "Welcome to Let's Play" sits directly on the busy photo. The blue "View upcoming events" button is boxed in red to mark it as the problem. Beside it are two contrast-checker panels. White on grey scores 2.35 to 1. Near-white on light blue scores 2.54 to 1. Both read Fail, with crosses against normal text, large text and graphics.

Slide 5: Use enough contrast between text and its background (continued)

On the slide: no text of its own.

Shown: the first event card, "Tuesday Stadium Run", with its date and location line boxed in red. That line is pale pink on white. On the right, the bad site's bright pink footer, and a contrast checker giving that pink on white 2.16 to 1, another Fail across the board.

Slide 6: Don't rely on colour to carry meaning

On the slide: the heading only.

Shown: two event cards side by side, Tuesday Stadium Run and Wednesday Sunset Yoga. Each has a small dot in its top right corner, boxed in red. One dot is green, one is red. That dot is the only thing telling you one event has spots available and the other is fully booked. There is no text label anywhere.

Slide 7: Don't rely on colour to carry meaning (continued)

On the slide: the same heading.

Shown: the identical pair of cards with all colour removed. Both dots are now the same mid-grey and indistinguishable. The two slides are shown together so that the same pair of cards can be seen with and without colour.

2. Touch target

Slide 8: Make interactive elements at least 24 by 24 pixels

On the slide: "2. Touch target", then the heading.

Shown: two failures marked with large red crosses. The bright pink footer with three tiny social media icons crammed together in a red box, and the hero's three carousel dots, also boxed. Both are well under the minimum size.

3. Text alternatives

Slide 9: Write alt text that describes the image's purpose

On the slide: "3. Text alternatives", then the heading. Short and to the point. Descriptive enough to provide relevant content and context. Should not contain any extra or unnecessary information, and should not repeat information that is already provided in the text.

Shown: a photo of an outdoor yoga class on grass, several people resting in child's pose on mats. Two captions sit over it, each with a red cross. The first reads "IMG_2886.jpg" and the second reads "People doing yoga."

Slide 10: Write alt text that describes the image's purpose (continued)

On the slide: the caption, with a green tick: "A group outdoor yoga class on a grassy lawn, participants resting in child's pose on their mats."

Shown: the same yoga photo, now full width, with that caption boxed over it.

Slide 11: Use empty alt text for decorative images

On the slide: the heading, then this text. An alt text should be empty, alt="", when an image is purely decorative and provides no meaningful information to the user.

Shown: the site's open navigation menu, listing Home, Events, About, News, Contact Us and Login, each with a small blue icon beside it. Two captions: "Home icon" with a red cross, and empty quote marks with a green tick. The icons repeat what the link text already says, so they should be silent.

4. Names and labels

Slide 12: Give every button a meaningful label

On the slide: "4. Names and Labels", then the heading.

  1. Be specific, avoid vague labels like "Submit" or "Continue".
  2. Use exact phrases like "Send Message" or "Save Changes".
  3. Label action buttons with verbs or action words.
  4. Use code attributes like aria-label if your button relies on symbols or icons.

Shown: the bad site's hero again, with the three carousel dots boxed in red and a caption reading "Button", "Button", "Button" with a red cross.

Slide 13: Give every button a meaningful label (continued)

On the slide: the caption "Button", "Button", "Button" with a red cross.

Shown: the phone view of the site on the left with the dots boxed, and on the right the VoiceOver Form Controls rotor panel open. It lists fourteen entries. One reads "View upcoming events". The other thirteen all read "button".

The form controls rotor produces this same list live on the bad page, if you would rather show it than leave it as a screenshot.

Slide 14: Give every button a meaningful label (continued)

On the slide: no text of its own.

Shown: two event cards with their Register buttons boxed in red, and the VoiceOver Links rotor open alongside. The list reads: logo_1.svg, Register, Register, Register, Read More, Read More, Read More, logo_1-invert2.svg, bad, bad, bad, then Privacy Policy, Contact Us, and the whole navigation repeated twice. Nothing in it says where any of those links go.

This slide is about link text rather than buttons, so the heading above it does not quite cover what it shows. It would be worth raising with Adeline before the session.

Slide 15: Add an aria-label to icon-only buttons

On the slide: the heading, then this text. Icon-only buttons absolutely require an aria-label. Then a code sample, a button element with aria-label="Facebook link" wrapping an SVG marked aria-hidden="true".

Shown: the pink footer with its three social icons boxed in red, captioned "link", "link", "link" with a red cross.

Slide 16: Use visible labels on form fields, not placeholder text

On the slide: the heading only.

Shown: the bad Event Registration form. Three input boxes hold only faint grey placeholder text, reading Full Name star, Email star and Phone, and all three are boxed in red. Below them are a date question with three pill buttons, a Terms and Conditions checkbox, a "Slide to verify" human check, a Complete Registration button, and "* = required" in small pink text at the bottom.

Slide 17: Use visible labels on form fields, not placeholder text (continued)

On the slide: no text beyond the two labels, "Do" with a green tick and "Don't" with a red cross.

Shown: two versions of the same short sign-in form. On the left, visible labels sit above each field: Email address, Password. On the right, the same fields carry only placeholder text inside the box, which disappears as soon as you type.

Slide 18: Avoid ambiguous text

On the slide: the heading, then this text. When a screen reader encounters ambiguous text like "10/3", it may read it as a fraction, or confuse the month and the day depending on the user's regional settings. Below that is a caption reading "Tuesday, ten slash three" with a red cross, and the question "Is it 10th of March or 3rd of October?"

Shown: the Tuesday Stadium Run card with "Tue, 10/3," boxed in red inside its date line.

5. Semantics

Slide 19: Use real heading levels, not just large text

On the slide: "5. Semantics", then the heading.

  • Headings are NOT just font size.
  • One <h1> per page, then descend one level at a time.
  • Never fake one with styled text.
  • How big it looks is a separate decision from what level it is.

Shown: text only.

Slide 20: Use real heading levels, not just large text (continued)

On the slide: Many assistive technology users skim a page by jumping from heading to heading via the rotor. If headings are missing or out of order, they lose their primary way to scan the page.

Shown: the bad site with the VoiceOver headings rotor open over it. It finds only three headings, and all three are level 4: Upcoming Events, Athlete Spotlight, Latest News. There is no level 1 and no level 2 on the page.

Slide 21: Use real heading levels, not just large text (continued)

On the slide: four labels, each with a green tick: H1, H2, H2, H3.

Shown: two phone views of the improved site with the labels pinned to the elements they belong to. "Every Body Belongs" is marked H1, "Upcoming Events" is marked H2, and the event title "Tuesday Stadium Run" is marked H3. The levels descend one at a time from top to bottom.

Slide 22: Use landmark elements: header, nav, main, footer

On the slide: the heading, then these points:

  • Use proper named regions, so someone can jump straight to the part they want instead of swiping through everything above it.
  • For example <header>, <nav>, one <main> per page, <footer>.
  • Give a skip link as the first focusable element.

Shown: a simple page diagram in pastel blocks. A header strip across the top, a narrow nav column down the left, a large main area filling the middle, and a footer strip across the bottom.

Slide 23: Use landmark elements: header, nav, main, footer (continued)

On the slide: three labels reading <div>, each with a red cross.

Shown: the bad site's home page in a phone frame with red boxes drawn round the header strip, the hamburger button and the whole body of the page. Every one of them is an unnamed div, so there is nothing for the landmarks rotor to find.

Slide 24: Use landmark elements: header, nav, main, footer (continued)

On the slide: three labels with green ticks: <header>, <nav>, <main>.

Shown: the same page, same boxes, now drawn in green and labelled with the real elements instead of divs.

Slide 25: Use the real element instead of a styled div

On the slide: the heading, then these points:

  • If the browser has an element for it, use it.
  • A div in a costume gives you nothing: no announced role, no announced state, no keyboard behaviour, no focusability.

Then a caption reading: don't use a stylised <div>, with a red cross. Use a proper checkbox or radio button for selectors. An <input type="radio"> with aria-checked true or false.

Shown: the full registration form on the right, with the row of three date pills boxed in red. They look like a radio group but have none of the behaviour of one.

6. Focus

Slide 26: Show a visible focus ring on every interactive element

On the slide: "6. Focus", then the heading. You must always see where you are, the focus ring. Never outline: none without a replacement.

Shown: a small panel of plain controls, each drawn with a clear blue focus ring: a slider, a button, a radio button and a text field.

Slide 27: Keep the focus order the same as the visual order

On the slide: the heading, and a note with a red cross: focus skips the date selector, terms and conditions checkbox, and human verification.

Shown: the registration form with a purple line traced across it, numbered one to six, showing the real focus path. It goes logo, hamburger, then the three text fields, then jumps straight to Complete Registration. The date pills, the checkbox and the verification slider are never visited.

Slide 28: Make content behind an open overlay unreachable

On the slide: "Focus management:" then the heading.

Shown: the site's navigation panel open as a full overlay, with a close cross in its top right corner, listing Home, Events, About, News, Contact Us and Login.

On the bad page the markup for this panel sits at the very end of the document, so moving forward from the hamburger takes you through the whole page underneath it before you reach any of these links.

7. Carousels

Slide 29: Treat a carousel as a last resort

On the slide: "7. Carousels", then the heading.

  • Don't use one. Stack the content, or use a grid or a simple list.
  • If you must use one: don't auto-advance, manual only, no timer.
  • Use real button controls with visible text or a proper label, aria-current on the active one, and previous and next as well as dots.

Shown: the site's Latest News carousel on a cream background. One news item is visible, "Club wins regional blind football tournament", with a photo of a blind football match and a Read More link. Three dots underneath.

Slide 30: Carousel best practice

On the slide: "Carousel best practice", as a link.

Shown: an example carousel from elsewhere, a coastal landscape captioned "Travel to Southwest England and Paris". It has a clear play and pause control in the top left and large previous and next arrows in the top right, all visible on the image rather than hidden.

8. Gesture-based interaction

Slide 31: Don't make swiping or dragging the only way to do something

On the slide: "8. Gesture-based interaction", then the heading. Conflict with system gestures: screen reader users rely on specific swipe gestures to move their focus or read the screen.

Shown: text only.

Slide 32: Don't make swiping or dragging the only way to do something (continued)

On the slide: Swiping left or right on a slider often accidentally triggers a "next element" command instead of moving the slider.

Shown: the registration form with the "Verify you're human" section boxed in red. It is a track labelled "Slide to verify" with a small blue handle at the left end, and it can only be operated by dragging.

This is one for you to speak to directly, since you will already have met the verification slider during Task 1 and can describe what happened when you tried to get past it.

The header carries the logo and hamburger, then a photo of the guide runner and blindfolded athlete as a normal image with the text underneath it rather than on top: "Welcome to Let's Play", "Find your team, where everybody belongs", "A community sports club for people of all abilities", and a View upcoming events button.
