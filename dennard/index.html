How to use this page

Every slide has its own heading, numbered in order, so you can move through the deck by heading and always know where Adeline is. Under each heading you get what is written on the slide, then a description of what is shown on it.

Where a slide carries the same point a second time, through a screenshot, a rotor list or a good-example counterpart, its heading is the one above it with "continued" after it. That means you will hear the same heading two or three times in a row, which is deliberate: it tells you the point has not changed.

The slide pictures on this page are marked decorative and are skipped, because everything in them is written out in the text instead. Nothing on this page moves or auto-plays.

The two live sites are the bad version of Let's Play and the improved version.

Slide 3 — Discussion: what visual accessibility issues can you find?

On the slide: "Discussion:" then "What visual accessibility issues can you find?" Three minutes, with the person next to you:

  • Is there enough contrast between text and its background?
  • Am I relying on colour alone to communicate meaning?
  • Are labels clear?
  • Are buttons and controls large enough to identify and target?
  • Can I tell where I need to click?
  • Can I tell whether something is selected, focused, disabled, or active?

Shown: text only.

Your demo starts after this slide. By this point the room has spent three minutes looking at the bad page, so they will have found the problems they can see. You pick it up from there.

Your demo

The demo has four parts. Adeline will prompt you at the start of each one, and you work through it out loud while the room watches. Everything below happens on the bad page. The notes under each part describe what the page will actually do, so that you can name each problem as you meet it rather than having to go looking for it.

Part 1 — First impressions of the home page

Adeline asks: can you open the home page and tell us what you understand from it?

What you will run into:

  • The logo announces as "logo underscore one dot s v g". The filename is the alt text.
  • The hamburger announces as a bare "button". No name, and no indication of whether it is open or closed.
  • The hero is a three-slide carousel that advances on its own, so whatever you are reading gets replaced underneath you.
  • Nothing is a heading until well down the page, and the first ones you reach are level 4 with no level 1 or 2 above them.
  • There are no landmarks at all, so there is no way to jump to the events or the footer.
  • The event photos have no alt attribute, so you may hear the image path read out.
  • Three links all say "Register" and three more all say "Read More".
  • The three social links in the footer announce as "link", "link", "link".
  • The whole navigation appears twice in the links list, because there are two copies of it in the page.

Part 2 — Task 1: find Saturday Reservoir Run and register for Sat, 5 Jun

What you will run into, in order:

  • The three events are a stack of plain containers, not a list, so nothing announces "list, 3 items" and there is no boundary between one event and the next. Working out which "Register" belongs to Saturday Reservoir Run means reading everything in between.
  • Its dates read as "Sat, five slash six, twelve slash six, nineteen slash six", and the middle dots between the date, the time and the location get spoken as punctuation.
  • On the registration page, the three form fields have no labels. The only hint is placeholder text, which also carries the asterisk that marks a field required, so once you type anything that information is gone.
  • The three date options look like radio buttons but are plain containers. There is no group, no announced count, and no way to tell which one is selected, even though Sat 5/6 is already picked by default.
  • The terms and conditions checkbox is also a plain container, so it never says checked or unchecked.
  • The "Verify you're human" control has to be dragged sideways, which fights the swipe you use to move between elements.
  • Submitting shows a confirmation on screen that is never announced, so there is no way to know whether it worked.

Part 3 — Task 2: find the news story "Volunteer coaches needed for spring term"

What you will run into:

  • It is the third of three news items, and the news section is a carousel that rotates roughly every second.
  • The only controls are three dots, and they are unlabelled buttons that all announce identically, with nothing marking which one is current.
  • Choosing a dot by hand does not stop the rotation, so you get pulled off the item a moment after you reach it.
  • There is no pause, stop or hide control anywhere.

This is the easiest part for the room to follow, because the timer moves the content on screen at the same moment it moves under you.

Part 4 — Task 3: open the navigation and go to Contact Us

What you will run into:

  • The hamburger is a container dressed up as a button. You can reach it and open it, but it announces as a bare "button" with no name and no open or closed state, so nothing tells you the menu opened.
  • Opening it does not move focus. The menu markup sits at the very end of the page, so moving forward from the hamburger takes you into the hero, the events, the spotlight and the news, all of which the menu is visually covering, before you ever reach its links.
  • None of that content behind the panel is made unreachable, so you are reading a page that sighted people in the room cannot see.
  • When you do get there, Contact Us appears twice, because the navigation exists twice in the page.

1. Colour

Slide 4 — Use enough contrast between text and its background

On the slide: "1. Colour", then the heading. Contrast ratios should be at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text.

Shown: the bad site's hero, a photo of two runners, one a blindfolded athlete running with a guide. White text "Welcome to Let's Play" sits directly on the busy photo, and the blue "View upcoming events" button is boxed in red to mark it as the problem. Beside it, two contrast-checker panels: white on grey scores 2.35 to 1, and near-white on light blue scores 2.54 to 1. Both read Fail, with crosses against normal text, large text and graphics.

Slide 5 — Use enough contrast between text and its background (continued)

On the slide: no text of its own.

Shown: the first event card, "Tuesday Stadium Run", with its date and location line boxed in red. That line is pale pink on white. On the right, the bad site's bright pink footer, and a contrast checker giving that pink on white 2.16 to 1, another Fail across the board.

Slide 6 — Don't rely on colour to carry meaning

On the slide: the heading only.

Shown: two event cards side by side, Tuesday Stadium Run and Wednesday Sunset Yoga. Each has a small dot in its top right corner, boxed in red. One dot is green, one is red. That dot is the only thing telling you one event has spots available and the other is fully booked. There is no text label anywhere.

Slide 7 — Don't rely on colour to carry meaning (continued)

On the slide: the same heading.

Shown: the identical pair of cards with all colour removed. Both dots are now the same mid-grey and indistinguishable. That is the point of showing the two slides together, because once the colour is taken away the information has gone with it.

2. Touch target

Slide 8 — Make interactive elements at least 24 by 24 pixels

On the slide: "2. Touch target", then the heading.

Shown: two failures marked with large red crosses. The bright pink footer with three tiny social media icons crammed together in a red box, and the hero's three carousel dots, also boxed. Both are well under the minimum size.

3. Text alternatives

Slide 9 — Write alt text that describes the image's purpose

On the slide: "3. Text alternatives", then the heading. Short and to the point. Descriptive enough to provide relevant content and context. Should not contain any extra or unnecessary information, and should not repeat information that is already provided in the text.

Shown: a photo of an outdoor yoga class on grass, several people resting in child's pose on mats. Two captions over it, each with a red cross: "IMG_2886.jpg" and "People doing yoga." One says nothing, the other says too little.

Slide 10 — Write alt text that describes the image's purpose (continued)

On the slide: the caption, with a green tick: "A group outdoor yoga class on a grassy lawn, participants resting in child's pose on their mats."

Shown: the same yoga photo, now full width, with that caption boxed over it.

Slide 11 — Use empty alt text for decorative images

On the slide: the heading, then: an alt text should be empty, alt="", when an image is purely decorative and provides no meaningful information to the user.

Shown: the site's open navigation menu, listing Home, Events, About, News, Contact Us and Login, each with a small blue icon beside it. Two captions: "Home icon" with a red cross, and empty quote marks with a green tick. The icons repeat what the link text already says, so they should be silent.

4. Names and labels

Slide 12 — Give every button a meaningful label

On the slide: "4. Names and Labels", then the heading.

  1. Be specific, avoid vague labels like "Submit" or "Continue".
  2. Use exact phrases like "Send Message" or "Save Changes".
  3. Label action buttons with verbs or action words.
  4. Use code attributes like aria-label if your button relies on symbols or icons.

Shown: the bad site's hero again, with the three carousel dots boxed in red and a caption reading "Button", "Button", "Button" with a red cross.

Slide 13 — Give every button a meaningful label (continued)

On the slide: the caption "Button", "Button", "Button" with a red cross.

Shown: the phone view of the site on the left with the dots boxed, and on the right the VoiceOver Form Controls rotor panel open. It lists fourteen entries. One reads "View upcoming events" and every other one reads simply "button". Thirteen identical entries in a row.

This is probably the strongest screen reader moment in the deck, so it is worth doing live if you can. Bringing up the form controls rotor on the bad page shows the problem more clearly than any explanation would.

Slide 14 — Give every button a meaningful label (continued)

On the slide: no text of its own.

Shown: two event cards with their Register buttons boxed in red, and the VoiceOver Links rotor open alongside. The list reads: logo_1.svg, Register, Register, Register, Read More, Read More, Read More, logo_1-invert2.svg, bad, bad, bad, then Privacy Policy, Contact Us, and the whole navigation repeated twice. Nothing in it says where any of those links go.

This slide is about link text rather than buttons, so the heading above it does not quite cover what it shows. It would be worth raising with Adeline before the session.

Slide 15 — Add an aria-label to icon-only buttons

On the slide: the heading, then: icon-only buttons absolutely require an aria-label. Then a code sample: a button element with aria-label="Facebook link" wrapping an SVG marked aria-hidden="true".

Shown: the pink footer with its three social icons boxed in red, captioned "link", "link", "link" with a red cross.

Slide 16 — Use visible labels on form fields, not placeholder text

On the slide: the heading only.

Shown: the bad Event Registration form. Three input boxes with only faint grey placeholder text inside them, Full Name star, Email star and Phone, all boxed in red. Below: a date question with three pill buttons, a Terms and Conditions checkbox, a "Slide to verify" human check, a Complete Registration button, and "* = required" in small pink text at the bottom.

Slide 17 — Use visible labels on form fields, not placeholder text (continued)

On the slide: no text beyond the two labels, "Do" with a green tick and "Don't" with a red cross.

Shown: two versions of the same short sign-in form. On the left, visible labels sit above each field: Email address, Password. On the right, the same fields carry only placeholder text inside the box, which vanishes the moment you start typing.

Slide 18 — Avoid ambiguous text

On the slide: the heading, then: when a screen reader encounters ambiguous text like "10/3", it may read it as a fraction or confuse the month and day depending on the user's regional settings. Then a caption: "Tuesday, ten slash three" with a red cross, and the question "Is it 10th of March or 3rd of October?"

Shown: the Tuesday Stadium Run card with "Tue, 10/3," boxed in red inside its date line.

5. Semantics

Slide 19 — Use real heading levels, not just large text

On the slide: "5. Semantics", then the heading.

  • Headings are NOT just font size.
  • One <h1> per page, then descend one level at a time.
  • Never fake one with styled text.
  • How big it looks is a separate decision from what level it is.

Shown: text only.

Slide 20 — Use real heading levels, not just large text (continued)

On the slide: Many assistive technology users skim a page by jumping from heading to heading via the rotor. If headings are missing or out of order, they lose their primary way to scan the page.

Shown: the bad site with the VoiceOver headings rotor open over it. It finds only three headings, and all three are level 4: Upcoming Events, Athlete Spotlight, Latest News. There is no level 1 and no level 2 on the page at all.

Slide 21 — Use real heading levels, not just large text (continued)

On the slide: four labels, each with a green tick: H1, H2, H2, H3.

Shown: two phone views of the improved site with the labels pinned to the elements they belong to. "Every Body Belongs" is marked H1, "Upcoming Events" is marked H2, and the event title "Tuesday Stadium Run" is marked H3. One level at a time, top to bottom.

Slide 22 — Use landmark elements: header, nav, main, footer

On the slide: the heading, then:

  • Use proper named regions, so someone can jump straight to the part they want instead of swiping through everything above it.
  • For example <header>, <nav>, one <main> per page, <footer>.
  • Give a skip link as the first focusable element.

Shown: a simple page diagram in pastel blocks. A header strip across the top, a narrow nav column down the left, a large main area filling the middle, and a footer strip across the bottom.

Slide 23 — Use landmark elements: header, nav, main, footer (continued)

On the slide: three labels reading <div>, each with a red cross.

Shown: the bad site's home page in a phone frame with red boxes drawn round the header strip, the hamburger button and the whole body of the page. Every one of them is an unnamed div, so there is nothing for the landmarks rotor to find.

Slide 24 — Use landmark elements: header, nav, main, footer (continued)

On the slide: three labels with green ticks: <header>, <nav>, <main>.

Shown: the same page, same boxes, now drawn in green and labelled with the real elements instead of divs.

Slide 25 — Use the real element instead of a styled div

On the slide: the heading, then:

  • If the browser has an element for it, use it.
  • A div in a costume gives you nothing: no announced role, no announced state, no keyboard behaviour, no focusability.

Then a caption: don't use a stylised <div>, with a red cross. Use a proper checkbox or radio button for selectors. An <input type="radio"> with aria-checked true or false.

Shown: the full registration form on the right, with the row of three date pills boxed in red. They look like a radio group and behave like nothing at all.

6. Focus

Slide 26 — Show a visible focus ring on every interactive element

On the slide: "6. Focus", then the heading. You must always see where you are, the focus ring. Never outline: none without a replacement.

Shown: a small panel of plain controls, each drawn with a clear blue focus ring: a slider, a button, a radio button and a text field.

Slide 27 — Keep the focus order the same as the visual order

On the slide: the heading, and a note with a red cross: focus skips the date selector, terms and conditions checkbox, and human verification.

Shown: the registration form with a purple line traced across it, numbered one to six, showing the real focus path. It goes logo, hamburger, then the three text fields, then jumps straight to Complete Registration. The date pills, the checkbox and the verification slider are never visited at all.

Slide 28 — Make content behind an open overlay unreachable

On the slide: "Focus management:" then the heading.

Shown: the site's navigation panel open as a full overlay, with a close cross in its top right corner, listing Home, Events, About, News, Contact Us and Login.

On the bad page the markup for this panel sits at the very end of the document, so moving forward from the hamburger takes you through the whole page underneath it before you reach any of these links.

7. Carousels

Slide 29 — Treat a carousel as a last resort

On the slide: "7. Carousels", then the heading.

  • Don't use one. Stack the content, or use a grid or a simple list.
  • If you must use one: don't auto-advance, manual only, no timer.
  • Use real button controls with visible text or a proper label, aria-current on the active one, and previous and next as well as dots.

Shown: the site's Latest News carousel on a cream background. One news item is visible, "Club wins regional blind football tournament", with a photo of a blind football match and a Read More link. Three dots underneath.

Slide 30 — Carousel best practice

On the slide: "Carousel best practice", as a link.

Shown: an example carousel from elsewhere, a coastal landscape captioned "Travel to Southwest England and Paris". It has a clear play and pause control in the top left and large previous and next arrows in the top right, all visible on the image rather than hidden.

8. Gesture-based interaction

Slide 31 — Don't make swiping or dragging the only way to do something

On the slide: "8. Gesture-based interaction", then the heading. Conflict with system gestures: screen reader users rely on specific swipe gestures to move their focus or read the screen.

Shown: text only.

Slide 32 — Don't make swiping or dragging the only way to do something (continued)

On the slide: Swiping left or right on a slider often accidentally triggers a "next element" command instead of moving the slider.

Shown: the registration form with the "Verify you're human" section boxed in red. It is a track labelled "Slide to verify" with a small blue handle at the left end, and it can only be operated by dragging.

This is one for you to speak to directly, since you will already have met the verification slider during Task 1 and can describe what happened when you tried to get past it.

Closing

Slide 33 — The improved site

On the slide: "The improved site". How might we make Let's Play more accessible and inclusive for visually impaired users? "Scan to access:" above a QR code.

Shown: a phone view of the improved site on a dark navy background. The header carries the logo and hamburger, then a photo of the guide runner and blindfolded athlete as a normal image with the text underneath it rather than on top: "Welcome to Let's Play", "Find your team, where everybody belongs", "A community sports club for people of all abilities", and a View upcoming events button. Upcoming Events follows below.

This is the second demo point. It is the same walkthrough as before but on the improved page, and the difference between the two is what the whole session has been building towards.
